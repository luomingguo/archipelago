Was this policy Pareto-improving? Topalova 2010 provides a causal test


 − Big idea: Exploit diferences in district-level exposure to tarif cuts to test how
   these cuts causally afect poverty and consumption

      □   Although all of India was subject to national tarif cuts, districts difered in their
          ‘exposure’ according to their industrial specialization at that time
      □   Districts with more agriculture are more ‘exposed’ because they are specialized in
          producing goods facing big tarif cuts
      □   Roughly 450 Indian districts, averaging 2m people per district. (Number of districts
          and population have risen since paper was written)

What should we expect to happen?


Almost certain – will occur under any scenario

 1. Price of goods in previously tarif-protected sectors will fall in price (towards world
    prices)
 2. Wages/earnings of workers in those sectors may also decline
 3. Consumer purchasing power will rise on average




                                                                                       21/31
What should we expect to happen?

Good scenario – what could go right
 1. Rise in purchasing power ofsets drop in incomes in tarif-protected sectors —
    leading to higher consumption

 2. Workers/households rapidly switch to new sectors, move to new districts

Bad scenario – what could go wrong
 1. Rise in purchasing power does not ofset drop in incomes in tarif-protected sectors
    — leading to lower consumption
 2. Workers/households do not rapidly switch to new sectors, districts

Bottom line of Topalova, 2010

Consequences of ‘shock therapy’ were shockingly bad

 1. Prices and wages/incomes fell in tarif-exposed industries
 2. Poverty rose in tarif-exposed districts, especially rural agricultural districts
 3. Household consumption fell in tarif-exposed districts
 4. Household consumption fell by most in the poorest households
 5. Workers/households did not move away from tarif-exposed regions
 6. Workers/households did not move out of tarif-exposed sectors

Concluding thoughts
The principle of comparative advantage is a fundamental economic insight — analogous
to the welfare theorems

 1. First welfare theorem shows that free trade among individuals is Pareto-improving
    (and leads to Pareto efcient allocations)
 2. The principle of comparative advantage says that allowing countries to trade
    always raises welfare in both countries
 3. Key diference: International trade does not beneft all individuals in each country
 4. Trade opening without lumpsum transfers yields both winners and losers

Second welfare theorem proves it is possible to make every citizen better of under trade
than autarky — but only if combined with lump-sum transfers                          31/31
