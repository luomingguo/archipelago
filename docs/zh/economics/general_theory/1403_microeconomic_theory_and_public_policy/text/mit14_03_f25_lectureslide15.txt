Trade and national income
Cross country comparisons


What does this fgure tell us about the causal efect of trade on national income? Probably
not much

 − The extent to which a country trades is endogenous

 − Countries that are rich for other reasons might trade more because they can aford to
   import more goods from overseas

 − Countries that pursue sound economic policies (i.e., that raise income) may also choose
   to pursue trade (another sound economic policy)

 − Countries that are rich in natural resources may trade because there is high world
   demand for their goods. But it may be their rich endowments that account for their
   wealth, not trade per se.

Trade and growth
Back to causal inference

 − We would like to measure the causal efect of trade on country j as follows:

                                        γj = YjT − YjA ,

    where Y is income per capita, γj is the causal efect of trade on Y in country j (γ
    stands for Gains from trade), and the superscripts A and T signify Autarky and Trade

 − Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference says that we cannot observe income per
   capita for country j both under both Autarky and free trade simultaneously

 − Can readily calculate
                             γ̂ = E Y T |T = 1 − E Y A |T = 0 ,

    where T ∈ {0, 1} indicates whether a country is open to free trade
 − But γ̂ is probably not a good estimate of γ ∗

Trade and growth
What can we do instead?



We need an “experiment” that exogenously raises or lowers trade in some group of countries

 − In the case of free trade, such experiments are difcult to fnd

 − What about unexpected events that suddenly open or close a country to trade (for
   example, war, natural disaster, revolutionary overthrow)?

 − Are these good quasi-experiments for this causal question?

 − Problem: they are likely to cause other economic and policy shocks in addition to trade
   that also directly raise or lower real income

Instrumental Variables (IV) —
        The big idea




                                7/41
Instrumental variables
 − The difculty of running an RCT or quasi-experiment motivates a subtle and powerful
   approach to identify causal efects: the method of Instrumental Variables (IV)

 − Here’s the idea: we are interested in measuring the efect of trade on income. Since
   trade is endogenous, we are reluctant to draw any causal inferences from the observed
   correlation
      1. Imagine hypothetically that there is some third variable Z ∈ {0, 1} that afects the extent to
         which countries trade
      2. And this variable is as good as randomly assigned to countries. That is, ‘treated’ and
         ‘untreated’ countries are comparable/ exchangeable
      3. Finally, we suspect that Z afects national income—if it afects it at all—only through its
         efect on trade

 − Under these assumptions, Z can serve as an “instrument” that exogenously manipulates
   trade, allowing us to study trade’s causal efect on income
                                                                                                  8/41
Instrumental variables: Core idea



                    The world has randomized something —

                   but it’s not exactly the thing you want



           Under an exclusion restriction, you may be able to “exploit”

     that available randomness and get (approximately) what you want anyway


                                                                              9/41
IV: Core idea




 − D is the treatment, Y is the outcome of interest, U is the unmeasured confounder (the
   source of endogeneity), and Z is the ‘instrument’

 − We would like to know the causal efect of D on Y . But U makes this a hard problem
 − The instrumental variable setup is as follows
      1. We have a frst-stage relationship: Z causally afects D
      2. We need an exclusion restriction: There is no efect of Z on Y that does not run through D
      3. Measure the reduced form relationship: the causal efect of Z on Y
      4. Estimate the causal efect of D on Y by comparing the causal efects of Z on D and Z on Y

An Instrument for trade

 − James Feyrer’s 2019 paper, “Trade and Income—Exploiting Time Series in Geography”

 − Proposes an ingenious Instrumental Variables (AKA, IV or 2SLS) approach for
   analyzing the causal efect of trade on national per capita income

 − Insight: Historically, most trade between non-contiguous countries occurred by sea

 − As the cost of air freight fell over the last four decades, countries began shipping (some)
   goods by airplane rather than ship

 − This cost decline diferentially reduced the cost of trade for countries whose trading
   routes involved circumnavigating large land masses (i.e., continents)

Key instrumental variables construct: The exclusion restriction
The exclusion restriction says
 − Instrument ASDD afects the outcome variable of interest only through its efect on the
   intermediating endogenous variable, ∆T

 − This implies that conditional on ∆Tj , the value of Aj is irrelevant

 − Can be expressed formally as follows, where c is some constant:

                     E [∆Yj |∆Tj = c, Aj = 1] = E [∆Yj |∆Tj = c, Aj = 0] ,
This postulate is untestable
 − Cannot manipulate ASDD for a given country, and moreover, if we could, this would
   also afect Tj (under our hypothesis above)

 − The exclusion restriction must be plausible or the IV strategy is a non-starter
The exclusion restriction might be falsifable                                        19/41
The ASDD idea — do we trust it?


Potential concerns

 − ASDD is not the only determinant of changing trading patterns

 − U.S. began trading extensively with China in the 1990s but was trading extensively with
   Japan decades earlier

 − That cannot be explained by air freight costs!

Is that a problem? Not necessarily. We require that:

 1. ASDD has a direct, measurable causal efect on trade

 2. ASDD does not plausibly afect national income through any other channel but trade

Putting IV to work: Four steps



 1. Balance of treatment and control groups

 2. First stage: Causal efect of the instrument (ASDD) on the endogenous variable (∆T )

 3. Reduced form: Causal efect of the instrument on the outcome variable ∆Y

 4. IV estimate: Causal efect of ∆T on ∆Y

Step 1: Balance of treatment and control groups

Treatment and control groups must be comparable—must have have balanced
counterfactual outcomes

 − Let Yjt equal the GDP of country j in time t
 − Imagine that there are two time periods, t = {0, 1}, and that in the early period t0 ,
   traded goods travel exclusively by sea, whereas in the latter, they can travel by air or sea
 − Let ∆Yj equal the change in GDP in country j between t = 0 and t = 1
 − For each country, two potential outcomes

                                      ∆Yj ∈ ∆Yj1 , ∆Yj0 ,

    where ∆Yj1 is change in GDP in j if Aj = 1, ∆Yj0 is change in GDP in j if Aj = 0.
We now face our standard exchangeability challenge
                                                                                            23/41
Step 1: Balance of treatment and control groups


 − Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference
      □   Each country j is one type or the other (ASDD either High or Low, A = 1 or A = 0)
      □   Cannot observe both ∆Yj1 and ∆Yj0

 − Nevertheless, this condition must be plausible — or it’s a no-go

                              E ∆Yj1 |A = 1      = E ∆Yj1 |A = 0

                              E ∆Yj0 |A = 1      = E ∆Yj0 |A = 0 .

 − If countries with high ASDD were ‘assigned’ low ASDD, their GDP growth would be
   the same as the countries that actually have low ASDD, and vice versa

                                       ∆Tj1 ≥ ∆Tj0 ∀ j,

Step 4: Estimating the causal efect of trade on GDP

 1. Let’s write the causal efect of ∆T on ∆Y as

                                 E [∆Yj |∆Tj ] = α + γ∆Tj

 2. We estimated the causal efect of ASDD on between 1960 and 1995 (frst stage)

                        π1 = E [∆Tj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Tj |Aj = 0] > 0

 3. We compared change in incomes of ASDD High and Low countries (second stage)

                          π2 = E [∆Yj |A = 1j ] − E [∆Yj |Aj = 0] .

Step 4: Estimating the causal efect of trade on GDP

We have one more step left...

 1. We estimated the causal efect of ASDD on between 1960 and 1995 (frst stage)

                          π1 = E [∆T |A = 1] − E [∆T |A = 0] > 0

 2. We compared change in incomes of ASDD High and Low countries (second stage)

                           π2 = E [∆Y |A = 1] − E [∆Y |A = 0] .

   Here, π2 is the causal efect of ASDD (not trade) on GDP

 3. Now, estimate γ̂ using the estimated causal relationships between (1) ASDD and ∆T,
    and (2) ASDD and ∆Y

Step 4 Estimating the causal efect of trade on GDP: IV method
 1. Causal efect of ASDD on Trade
                                          E [∆Tj |Aj = 1] = α1 + π1
                                          E [∆Tj |Aj = 0] = α1
                       E [∆Tj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Tj |Aj = 0] = π1
 2. Causal efect of ASDD on GDP growth
                                     E [∆Yj |Aj = 1] = α2 + π2
                                          E [∆Yj |Aj = 0] = α2
                       E [∆Yj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Yj |Aj = 0] = π2
 3. Substituting gives us expression for the causal efect of ASDD on GDP
      E [∆Yj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Yj |Aj = 0]     =    π2
                                            =    γ (E [∆Tj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Tj |Aj = 0])
                                            =    γ × π1
                                          π2 = γ × π1 −→ γ = π2 /π1
                                                                                         38/41
IV Algebra: Closing the loop



 − Combining our two causal efects estimates, π1 and π2 , we can estimate the causal efect
   of trade on income

                    E [∆Yj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Yj |Aj = 0]   π2   π1 × γ
                                                      =    =        = γ̂
                    E [∆Tj |Aj = 1] − E [∆Tj |Aj = 0]   π1     π1


 − We thus estimate the causal efect of trade on income by taking the ratio of the two
   causal efects
