Course Description

This class teaches modern tools and methods for product design and development. The

cornerstone is a project in which teams of management, engineering, and industrial

design students conceive, design, and prototype a new product or service.
 
The class is

primarily intended for Sloan MBA students (particularly the Entrepreneurship and

Innovation track and the Leaders for Global Operations program) and for MIT

engineering graduate students (particularly mechanical engineering and manufacturing

master’s program
s). The course is jointly taught with Rhode Island School of Design for

industrial design students as a senior studio.

Learning Objectives

The focus of Product Design and Development
 
is integration of the design, marketing,

engineering, and business functions of
 
a firm
 
in creating a new product. The course is

intended to provide you with the following benefits:

•
 
Competence with a set of tools and methods for product design and development

•
 
Confidence in your own abilities to create a new product (physical or digital).

•
 
Understanding of the many disciplines involved in creating a new product (e.g.,

strategy, product management, marketing, finance, industrial design, digital UX,

engineering, production, supply chain, environmental safety, project management).

•
 
Familiarity with agile development process roles, events, and artifacts used to

coordinate an interdisciplinary team to achieve a challenging objective.

•
 
Reinforcement of specific knowledge from other courses through practice and

reflection in an action

Your challenge in the project portion of this course is to design a new product and to

develop a prototype version of it.
 
The goal of this exercise is to learn principles and

methods of product development in a realistic context.
 
Most professionals work under

development process.
 
In this course, the project stress level will be low enough that

Guidelines for Projects

While special cases will be considered, you are strongly encouraged to propose

opportunities
 
and choose a project satisfying all of the following constraints:

•
 
There should be a
 
demonstrable market
 
for the product.
 
One good way to verify a

market need is to identify existing products that attempt to meet the need.
 
Your

product need not be a variant of an existing product, but the market need addressed

by your product should be clearly evident.
 
The product does not need to have a

tremendous economic potential, but should at least be an attractive opportunity for a

new venture, an established firm, or a non
-
profit with related products and/or skills.

•
 
Most products developed in this class are
 
material goods and not pure services
.

While the ideas in the course apply to services and software products (for example,

customer needs, agile development,
 
and
 
product architecture
), we have found that

having most teams develop physical products gives the class a common experience

and timeline we can use as a basis for class discussions and project reviews.

Nevertheless, we welcome project proposals from students interested in developing

software, services, mobile apps, and
 
platform
-
based enterprises.

•
 
Systems are too complex
 
to develop in this class.
 
In general, products should have a

high likelihood of containing fewer than
 
ten
 
parts.
 
Although you cannot anticipate the

design details, it is easy to anticipate that
 
a
 
cordless
 
hammer
 
drill will have more

than
 
ten
 
parts and that a garlic press can have fewer than
 
ten
.

•
 
You should be confident of being
 
able to prototype
 
the product for less than $1000

using largely the resources available at MIT and RISD.
 
For example, a new razor to

compete with Gillette and Harry’s may have about 10 parts, but would require tens of

thousands of dollars to create an accurate, functional, testable prototype.

•
 
The product should require
 
no technological breakthroughs
. (Yes, a safer airbag

would be
 
great, but can you do it without inventing a new chemical?)
 
You do not

have time to deal with large technological uncertainties.

•
 
Your team should have
 
access to more than
 
ten
 
potential users
 
of the product (more

than 20 would be nice).
 
For example, you would have great difficulty researching

agricultural irrigation systems without leaving Cambridge or Providence, so you

better have a plan for making effective, direct contact with customers.
